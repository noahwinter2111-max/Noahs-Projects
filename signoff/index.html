02

How it works

Make it. Then manage it.

Two jobs, one chat. Each step waits for the one before it, and anything that leaves the company waits for you.

Make it

From a sentence to a company

  1. Tell it. What you do, where you live, whether you'll raise money.
  2. Prove it's you. ID and a selfie. Signoff never keeps your ID number.
  3. Form it. The filing and the tax ID, after you tap approve.
  4. Bank it. Your papers go to the bank. The bank opens the account.
  5. Connect it. Books and email, so everything lands in one place.

Manage it

Then it runs, and asks first

  1. Bills. Paid on their due dates. Anything over your limit waits for you.
  2. Money in. Invoices, payment links, subscriptions. Reminders go out by themselves.
  3. Taxes. A share of every deposit saved. Quarterly tax paid. Forms filed.
  4. People. Pay contractors, pay yourself, cards with a limit.
  5. Investors. Raise money, keep it apart, keep the cap table.

What it runs

One chat. The whole back office.

Everything below works in the prototype today. Reading is free. Sending anything out needs a tap. Moving money needs a six-digit code.

Company

Identity check, formation in any state or Delaware, EIN, documents in a vault.

Bank

Pay people with a code, standing payments, cards locked to one kind of spending, the balance always in view.

Bills

Bills arrive from your inbox, get paid on their due dates or by autopay, and anything over your limit is held.

Getting paid

Invoices that chase themselves, payment links, monthly subscriptions, refunds with a code.

Books

QuickBooks with the bank feed on, every transaction categorised, profit and loss on demand.

Email

Reads, drafts and replies from your own address. Refuses anything that tries to redirect a payment.

Taxes

A share of every deposit reserved, quarterly payments to the IRS, the annual report, 1099s, a CPA who files.

Investors

C corp conversion, SAFEs for signature, money kept in a raised account, cap table, runway.

Activity

Every proposal, what tier it was, and whether you approved, declined, or the gate refused it.

03

Who decides

It does the work. You hold the pen.

The model proposes. A policy gate written in plain code decides what each action needs. The model can't argue its way past it, and neither can a prompt hidden in an email.

What Signoff does

  • Reads your inbox, books and balance on its own.
  • Drafts filings, invoices and emails, then waits for your tap.
  • Sends a payment to the bank only after you type the six-digit code.
  • Keeps a log of every proposal, approval and decline.
  • Issues SAFEs and keeps the cap table when you raise.

What it will not do

  • Hold your money. The bank holds it; Signoff only sends instructions.
  • Sign anything for you. Every filing and payment carries your approval.
  • Ask for your SSN, ID number or bank password. The partners collect those directly.
  • Pay a new payee more than $1,500, or more than your daily limit, without a passkey.
  • Issue a SAFE with a cap under four times the amount, or over $5M. Those go to a lawyer.

05

Questions

Things people ask before they sign up.

How is this different from CoFounder.AI and the other AI assistants?

They are advisors. They plan, brainstorm and draft, and you go off and do the real work somewhere else. Signoff does the real work: it files with the state, gets the EIN, opens the bank account, keeps the books and moves the money, with your sign-off on every outward step. If you want someone to talk strategy with, use one of them. If you want the company to exist by tonight, use Signoff.

Is this a real product?

Not yet. This page is a working prototype of the product. The conversation runs on a script by default, or on Claude if you add your own API key in Settings. The formation partner, identity check, bank, QuickBooks and Gmail are all simulated so you can click through the whole journey in about three minutes.

Why does the bank do the identity check instead of you?

Because the bank has to do it anyway, by law, and it's liable for the result. Doing it twice costs money and annoys the owner. Signoff sends the bank the documents it needs and waits for the bank's answer.

What stops the model from doing something it shouldn't?

It can only act through a fixed list of tools, and each tool goes through a gate written in ordinary code. The gate knows which stage you're at, what tier the action is, and what approval it needs. Read-only actions run freely. Reversible writes run and are logged. Anything that leaves the company, like a filing, an email or an invoice, waits for your tap. Anything that moves money also needs a one-time code, and the bank's own checks sit on top of that.

What does it cost to run?

The expensive part is the model, so the agent only gets the tools for the stage you're in and keeps its answers short. A typical setup conversation is a few hundred thousand tokens end to end. Partner fees, like the state filing fee, are passed through at cost and shown on the card before you approve.

Can I bring my own bank or accountant?

Later. The first version works with one bank and QuickBooks Online, because a single integration that works end to end is worth more than four that half work. Gmail and Microsoft 365 are both supported for email from the start.

Is this for startups or small businesses?

Both, and the difference is one answer at the start. A founder who plans to raise gets a Delaware C corporation with founder shares, because that's what investors and SAFEs expect, and pays Delaware franchise tax for it. Everyone else gets a home-state LLC, which is cheaper and simpler. If a small business later decides to raise, Signoff converts it, with your approval.

Who is building this?

An independent project. Evan Winter is the founder of record: he holds the accounts, signs the contracts and put in the first money, because an officer of record has to be a named adult. The product, the design and the plan are built independently, self-funded, with no outside investors yet.
